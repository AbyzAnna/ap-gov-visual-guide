Topic 2.6

Expansion of Presidential Power

Essential QuestionHow have presidents interpreted and explained their use of formal and informal powers?
In one minute

Presidential power has grown far beyond the framers' design, especially through wars, economic crises, and the stewardship theory. Federalist No. 70 (Hamilton) defended a single, 'energetic' executive. Critics warn of an imperial presidency; Congress responded with the War Powers Act (1973) and the 22nd Amendment (1951).

1Federalist No. 70

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Federalist No. 70

Alexander Hamilton · 1788 · Required foundational document
Big argument: A single executive gives the government 'energy' — decisiveness, speed, and accountability — that a council or plural executive could not.
  • 'Energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.'
  • Ingredients of energy: unity, duration, adequate support, competent powers.
  • A single president is accountable — the public knows whom to blame.
  • A plural executive would bring conflict and hide responsibility.
  • Anti-Federalists feared this would create a king; Hamilton argued elections and checks would restrain it.

“Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.”

2How Presidents Expanded the Office

1789–97
George Washington — set precedents: cabinet, two-term tradition, neutrality proclamation
1829–37
Andrew Jackson — used the veto for policy reasons; claimed to represent the people directly
1861–65
Abraham Lincoln — suspended habeas corpus, Emancipation Proclamation, used war powers broadly
1901–09
Theodore Roosevelt — stewardship theory: president may do anything not forbidden by law; 'bully pulpit'
1913–21
Woodrow Wilson — led legislative agenda; delivered State of the Union in person
1933–45
Franklin D. Roosevelt — New Deal, huge bureaucracy, four terms → 22nd Amendment
1960s–70s
Vietnam & Watergate → 'imperial presidency' (Arthur Schlesinger); War Powers Act (1973)
2001–
War on Terror: surveillance, drone strikes, broad use of executive orders

Stewardship theory (T. Roosevelt)

  • President is the 'steward of the people'
  • May do anything the Constitution does NOT forbid
  • Broad, active presidency
VS

Constitutional / literalist theory (Taft)

  • President may only use powers clearly granted
  • Narrow, restrained presidency

3Limits Added Over Time

22nd Amendment (1951)

Limits presidents to two elected terms (max 10 years) — a reaction to FDR.

War Powers Act (1973)

Notify Congress within 48 hours; withdraw in 60 days without authorization (+30 for withdrawal). Presidents argue it's unconstitutional and often ignore it.

Budget & Impoundment Control Act (1974)

Stopped presidents from refusing to spend appropriated money; created CBO.

“The imperial presidency, created by wars abroad, has made a bold bid for power at home.”

— Arthur Schlesinger, 1973
