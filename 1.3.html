Topic 1.3

Government Power and Individual Rights

Essential QuestionHow are Federalist and Anti-Federalist views on central government and democracy reflected in America's foundational documents?
In one minute

The ratification debate pitted the Federalists (strong national government, large republic controls factions) against the Anti-Federalists (power should stay with states and the people; a large republic will become tyrannical). Their arguments are captured in Federalist No. 10 (Madison) and Brutus No. 1. The Anti-Federalists lost the ratification fight but won the Bill of Rights.

1Opposing Beliefs

FEDERALISTS

  • Leaders: Madison, Hamilton, John Jay
  • Wanted a strong central government
  • A large republic controls factions
  • Bill of Rights unnecessary (even dangerous — implies unlisted rights don't exist)
  • Wrote The Federalist Papers (85 essays under the name 'Publius')
VS

ANTI-FEDERALISTS

  • Leaders: Patrick Henry, George Mason, 'Brutus' (likely Robert Yates)
  • Feared a powerful, distant national government
  • Liberty survives only in small republics
  • Demanded a Bill of Rights
  • Feared the necessary and proper and supremacy clauses

2Federalist No. 10 — Controlling Factions

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Federalist No. 10

James Madison · 1787 · Required foundational document
Big argument: Factions are inevitable because they come from human nature; you can't remove the causes without destroying liberty, so you must control the effects — and a large republic does this best.
  • Faction = a group of citizens united by a common interest adverse to the rights of others or the public good.
  • The most common cause of faction is 'the various and unequal distribution of property.'
  • Two cures for the causes: destroy liberty (worse than the disease) or give everyone the same opinions (impossible).
  • So control the effects: a minority faction is beaten by majority vote; a majority faction is checked by a large republic with many competing interests — no single faction can dominate.
  • Representatives 'refine and enlarge the public views' — filter passions (republicanism).

“Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires.”

3Brutus No. 1 — Warning Against a Large Republic

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Brutus No. 1

Anti-Federalist essay · 1787 · Required foundational document
Big argument: A large republic cannot work: the national government will grow too powerful, swallow up the states, and lose touch with the people.
  • Citing Montesquieu: a free republic must be small, with people who share interests.
  • The necessary and proper clause and supremacy clause give Congress nearly unlimited power — it could abolish state governments.
  • Congress's unlimited power to tax will drain the states.
  • Representatives would be too few and too far from the people to know their needs.
  • Without a bill of rights, liberties are unprotected.

“In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar. If this be not the case, there will be a constant clashing of opinions.”

AP Exam Tip

The AP argument essay often asks you to use Federalist 10, Brutus 1, or Federalist 51 as evidence. Know the main claim of each in one sentence.

Remember

Compromise outcome: Federalists promised a Bill of Rights to win ratification in key states (Massachusetts, Virginia, New York); Madison introduced it in the First Congress, ratified 1791.
