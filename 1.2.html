Topic 1.2

Types of Democracy

Essential QuestionHow are models of representative democracy visible in U.S. institutions, policies, events, and debates?
In one minute

The U.S. is a representative democracy, but three competing models describe how it actually works: participatory (broad citizen involvement), pluralist (groups compete for influence), and elite (a small educated/wealthy group holds influence). All three exist in American government today, and the tension between them is a constant theme.

1Three Models of Representative Democracy

MANY people

Participatory democracy

Broad participation in politics and civil society by many citizens. Examples: initiatives and referendums in states, town-hall meetings, voting, protests, the direct primary.

GROUPS

Pluralist democracy

Group-based activism by citizens with common interests who compete to influence policy. Examples: interest groups like the NRA, AARP, Sierra Club; Madison's competing factions in Federalist 10.

FEW people

Elite democracy

Limited participation; a small, well-educated or wealthy group has the most influence. Examples: the Electoral College, Senate originally chosen by state legislatures, large campaign donors, Supreme Court appointed for life.

3Tension Over the Models

The framers mixed the models on purpose. They feared 'mob rule' by an uninformed majority (so they built in elite features), yet believed in popular sovereignty (so the House is directly elected). Debates today echo the tension:

  • Should the Electoral College be replaced by a national popular vote? (elite vs participatory)
  • Does money in politics let wealthy donors dominate? (elite vs pluralist)
  • Do interest groups represent ordinary people, or mostly organized, well-funded ones? (pluralist critique)
  • Do low voter turnout and polarization weaken participatory democracy?

The Federalists favored a stronger elite element and a large republic of many factions (pluralism); the Anti-Federalists favored participatory, local democracy where representatives stay close to the people.

4Representative Democracy in the United States Today

  • Expansion of suffrage (15th, 19th, 24th, 26th Amendments) moved the U.S. toward participatory democracy.
  • The 17th Amendment (1913) — direct election of senators — reduced an elite feature.
  • Many states allow initiatives (citizens propose a law by petition) and referendums (citizens vote on a law/measure) — there is no national referendum.
  • Interest groups number in the thousands, and the internet makes organizing easier (pluralism).
AP Exam Tip

FRQ scenarios often describe an event (a ballot measure, an interest group lobbying, the Electoral College) and ask you to identify the model. Look for: who participates and how many.
