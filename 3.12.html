Topic 3.12

Balancing Minority and Majority Rights

Essential QuestionHow has the Supreme Court allowed the restriction of the civil rights of minorities and at other times protected those rights?
In one minute

The Court has both restricted minority rights (Plessy, the Civil Rights Cases, Korematsu) and protected them (Brown, Swann, Shaw). Implementing Brown was slow: Southern states used 'freedom-of-choice plans' to avoid integrating, until the Court approved busing in Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg (1971). Majority-minority districts raise similar questions in voting.

  • Shaw v. Reno (1993) — limits on racial gerrymanders

Supporting case

Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education (1971)

9–0
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection
Facts
Despite Brown, Charlotte, North Carolina schools remained largely segregated because of housing patterns.
Constitutional question
Can federal courts order busing to achieve racial integration?
Decision
Yes. Chief Justice Burger, unanimously: busing, racial ratios, and redrawn attendance zones are acceptable tools to remedy segregation.
Why it matters
Gave courts power to actively enforce Brown; sparked fierce public opposition to 'forced busing.'
Memory hook: Swann = School buses integrate.
AP Exam Tip

Comparison FRQ: Brown (required) vs Swann or Plessy (non-required). Same clause (equal protection) — Plessy restricted rights; Brown and Swann protected them.

3Majority-Minority Districts

Under the Voting Rights Act, states drew majority-minority districts to give minority voters a real chance to elect representatives of their choice. But in Shaw v. Reno (1993) the Court said race can't be the predominant factor. The tension: protecting minority voting power vs avoiding racial classification.

“Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.”

— Justice John Marshall Harlan, dissenting in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)
