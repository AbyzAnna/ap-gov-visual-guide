Topic 2.10

The Court in Action

Essential QuestionHow have changes in the Supreme Court over time led to debates about the legitimacy of the court?
In one minute

The Court's direction changes with its members — from the activist Warren Court to the Burger and Rehnquist Courts to today's conservative Roberts Court. Justices' philosophies (strict constructionist vs liberal constructionist) shape rulings. Cases reach the Court by a petition for certiorari; the rule of four decides which are heard. Decisions come with majority, concurring, and dissenting opinions.

1An Evolving Court

1801–35
Marshall Court — strengthens national power: Marbury, McCulloch, Gibbons
1953–69
Warren Court — civil rights & liberties revolution: Brown, Mapp, Gideon, Miranda, Engel, Tinker, Baker v. Carr
1969–86
Burger Court — Roe v. Wade, Bakke, U.S. v. Nixon, busing (Swann)
1986–2005
Rehnquist Court — federalism revival: Lopez, Printz; Bush v. Gore
2005–
Roberts Court — Heller, McDonald, Citizens United, Obergefell, Dobbs, SFFA v. Harvard

2Judicial Philosophies

Strict constructionist (originalist)

  • Interpret the Constitution as written / as originally understood
  • Leave policy to elected branches
  • Usually associated with conservatives (Scalia, Thomas)
VS

Liberal (loose) constructionist

  • 'Living Constitution' that adapts to new times
  • Willing to recognize rights not explicitly listed
  • Usually associated with liberals (Ginsburg, Sotomayor)

Majority opinion

The Court's official decision and reasoning — becomes precedent.

Concurring opinion

Agrees with the outcome but for different or extra reasons.

Dissenting opinion

Disagrees with the majority; may influence future cases (e.g., Harlan's Plessy dissent).

Amicus curiae

'Friend of the court' brief filed by an outside group.

4Legislating After Unfavorable Decisions

Congress and the states can respond to rulings: by passing new laws (the Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act, 2009, reversed Ledbetter v. Goodyear, 2007), by proposing amendments (the 26th Amendment responded to Oregon v. Mitchell, 1970), or by rewriting laws to fit a ruling (the Gun-Free School Zones Act was amended after Lopez).

AP Exam Tip

Comparing a required case to a non-required one is a common FRQ (SCOTUS comparison question). Identify the shared clause, then explain why the facts led to the same or different outcomes.
