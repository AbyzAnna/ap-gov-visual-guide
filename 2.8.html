Topic 2.8

The Judicial Branch

Essential QuestionHow does the principle of judicial review check the power of the other branches and state government?
In one minute

Article III creates the Supreme Court and lets Congress create lower courts. Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton) calls the judiciary the 'least dangerous' branch and defends life tenure and judicial independence. Marbury v. Madison (1803) established judicial review — the power to strike down unconstitutional laws. The federal courts form a three-level system.

1Constitutional Authority

Article III: judicial power is vested 'in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish.' Judges serve 'during good Behaviour' (life tenure) and their salaries can't be cut. The Judiciary Act of 1789 created the lower courts and the Attorney General office.

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Federalist No. 78

Alexander Hamilton · 1788 · Required foundational document
Big argument: The judiciary is the 'least dangerous' branch because it has 'neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment' — so it needs independence (life tenure) to protect the Constitution.
  • Executive holds the sword; legislature holds the purse; courts hold neither.
  • Life tenure ensures independence from political pressure.
  • Courts must declare acts contrary to the Constitution void — the Constitution is fundamental law (an early argument for judicial review).
  • The judiciary protects individual rights against temporary majorities.

“The judiciary... has no influence over either the sword or the purse... It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment.”

2A Three-Level System

U.S. Supreme Court

9 justices; mostly appellate; chooses ~60–80 cases a year via certiorari; decisions are binding nationwide

▼

U.S. Circuit Courts of Appeals

13 circuits (12 regional + Federal Circuit); panels of 3 judges; no juries; review trial records

▼

U.S. District Courts

94 trial courts; original jurisdiction; juries; witnesses & evidence

Original jurisdiction

  • Court hears the case FIRST
  • Determines facts (trial)
  • District courts; Supreme Court only in cases involving states or ambassadors
VS

Appellate jurisdiction

  • Court reviews a lower court's decision
  • Looks at legal errors, not new facts
  • Courts of Appeals; most Supreme Court cases

State courts handle most cases (criminal, family, contracts). A case can move from a state supreme court to the U.S. Supreme Court only if it raises a federal or constitutional question.

3Required Case: Marbury v. Madison

REQUIRED CASE

Marbury v. Madison (1803)

4–0
Article III · Judiciary Act of 1789
Facts
On his way out, President John Adams appointed William Marbury as a justice of the peace ('midnight judges'). The commission wasn't delivered. New Secretary of State James Madison (under Jefferson) refused to deliver it. Marbury asked the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus under the Judiciary Act of 1789.
Constitutional question
Does the Supreme Court have the power to order Madison to deliver the commission?
Decision
Chief Justice John Marshall: Marbury deserved his commission, but the part of the Judiciary Act giving the Supreme Court power to issue the writ in its original jurisdiction expanded Article III unconstitutionally — so it was void. The Court can't issue the writ.
Why it matters
Established judicial review: courts can declare acts of Congress unconstitutional. Made the judiciary a co-equal branch.
Memory hook: Marbury = Marshall Makes judicial review. Lost the battle (no writ), won the war (power).
AP Exam Tip

Connect Marbury to Federalist 78 — Hamilton argued for judicial review before Marshall made it official.
