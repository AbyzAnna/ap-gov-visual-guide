Topic 3.10

Social Movements and Equal Protection

Essential QuestionHow have constitutional provisions supported and motivated social movements?
In one minute

The equal protection clause of the 14th Amendment ('no state shall... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws') has inspired movements for African Americans, women, and LGBTQ Americans. Leaders like Martin Luther King Jr. ("Letter from a Birmingham Jail") used nonviolent civil disobedience; women won the Nineteenth Amendment (1920), the Equal Pay Act (1963) and Title IX (1972); LGBTQ activists won Lawrence, Obergefell and Bostock.

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Letter from a Birmingham Jail

Martin Luther King Jr. · April 1963 · Required foundational document
Big argument: Unjust laws must be broken openly and nonviolently, with willingness to accept the penalty; waiting for gradual change means justice denied.
  • Written in response to eight white clergymen who called the protests 'unwise and untimely.'
  • 'Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.'
  • A just law squares with moral law; an unjust law degrades human personality (segregation laws).
  • Four steps of nonviolent campaigns: collect facts, negotiate, self-purification, direct action.
  • Criticized the 'white moderate' who preferred order to justice.
  • 'Justice too long delayed is justice denied.'

4Levels of Scrutiny Under Equal Protection

Strict scrutiny

Race, national origin, fundamental rights — law must serve a compelling interest and be narrowly tailored (almost always struck down)

▼

Intermediate scrutiny

Sex/gender — law must serve an important interest, substantially related

▼

Rational basis

Age, wealth, most other laws — any legitimate interest, reasonably related (usually upheld)
