Topic 1.8

Constitutional Interpretations of Federalism

Essential QuestionHow has a balance of power between national and state governments been interpreted over time?
In one minute

The Constitution's words on federalism are broad — the necessary and proper (elastic) clause, the commerce clause, the supremacy clause, and the Tenth Amendment — so the Supreme Court decides the balance. McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) expanded national power through implied powers and supremacy; United States v. Lopez (1995) set a limit on the commerce clause and protected state power.

1The Constitutional Tug-of-War

Clauses that EXPAND national power

  • Necessary and proper (elastic) clause — Art. I, Sec. 8: Congress may make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers → implied powers
  • Commerce clause — regulate commerce among the states
  • Supremacy clause — Art. VI: federal law beats state law
  • 14th Amendment (1868) — limits states: due process & equal protection
VS

Clauses that PROTECT state power

  • Tenth Amendment (1791) — powers not delegated to the U.S. are reserved to the states or the people
  • Enumerated powers — Congress has only the powers listed
  • 11th Amendment — limits suits against states

2Required Case: McCulloch v. Maryland

REQUIRED CASE

McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

9–0
Necessary and proper clause · Supremacy clause
Facts
Congress created the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland taxed the bank's Baltimore branch; cashier James McCulloch refused to pay.
Constitutional question
Can Congress create a national bank when that power is not listed? Can a state tax a federal institution?
Decision
Yes and no. Chief Justice John Marshall: the necessary and proper clause gives Congress implied powers to carry out enumerated ones (taxing, borrowing, regulating commerce). And because of the supremacy clause, a state cannot tax the national government — 'the power to tax involves the power to destroy.'
Why it matters
Established implied powers and national supremacy; laid the foundation for a strong national government and broad congressional power.
Memory hook: McCulloch = More power for the Main (national) government. 'Power to tax is the power to destroy.'

3Commerce Clause: Expanding Then Limiting

1824
Gibbons v. Ogden — 'commerce' is broad; includes navigation; federal law beats state steamboat monopoly.
1930s–40s
After FDR's court fight, the Court lets Congress regulate almost any economic activity (e.g., Wickard v. Filburn, 1942 — wheat grown for your own farm).
1964
Heart of Atlanta Motel v. U.S. — Civil Rights Act's ban on discrimination upheld under the commerce clause.
1995
United States v. Lopez — FIRST limit in ~60 years: guns near schools are not commerce.
2000
U.S. v. Morrison — Violence Against Women Act civil remedy struck down; same logic as Lopez.
2012
NFIB v. Sebelius — Affordable Care Act mandate upheld as a tax, not under commerce; Medicaid expansion couldn't be coerced.

Other doctrines: dual federalism (pre-1937 view that state and national spheres are separate) and selective exclusiveness (Cooley v. Board of Wardens, 1851 — some commerce issues need one national rule, others can be left to states).

4Required Case: United States v. Lopez

REQUIRED CASE

United States v. Lopez (1995)

5–4
Commerce clause · Tenth Amendment
Facts
Alfonso Lopez, a 12th-grader in San Antonio, brought a handgun to school and was charged under the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act (1990), which Congress passed using its commerce power.
Constitutional question
Does the commerce clause allow Congress to ban guns near schools?
Decision
No. Chief Justice Rehnquist: carrying a gun near a school is not economic activity and does not 'substantially affect' interstate commerce. Upholding the law would give Congress a general police power that belongs to the states.
Why it matters
First time since the New Deal that the Court limited the commerce clause — a win for federalism / states' rights and limits on Congress.
Memory hook: Lopez = Limits on Congress. Guns in school ≠ commerce.

5National Concerns, State Obligations

Congress can also pressure states through money (see 1.7) and through the 14th Amendment's guarantees. The Court has said Congress may not commandeer state officials — Printz v. U.S. (1997) struck down a Brady Act requirement that local sheriffs run background checks.

AP Exam Tip

Classic FRQ comparison: a non-required case about commerce or federalism compared with Lopez or McCulloch. Ask: did the ruling expand or limit federal power?
