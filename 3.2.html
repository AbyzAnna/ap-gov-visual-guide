Topic 3.2

First Amendment: Freedom of Religion

Essential QuestionTo what extent does the Supreme Court's interpretation of freedom of religion reflect a commitment to individual liberty?
In one minute

The First Amendment has two religion clauses: the establishment clause (government can't establish or favor a religion — Jefferson's 'wall of separation') and the free exercise clause (government can't prohibit practicing your religion). Engel v. Vitale (1962) banned school-sponsored prayer; Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972) protected Amish families from compulsory schooling past 8th grade.

1Two Religion Clauses

“Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof...”

— First Amendment

Establishment clause

  • Government can't create a national church
  • Can't favor one religion or religion over non-religion
  • Jefferson: 'a wall of separation between church and state'
  • Cases: Engel v. Vitale, Lemon v. Kurtzman, Abington v. Schempp
VS

Free exercise clause

  • Government can't prohibit religious practice
  • Beliefs are absolutely protected; actions can be limited
  • Cases: Wisconsin v. Yoder, Reynolds v. U.S. (polygamy not protected), Employment Division v. Smith (1990)
Common mistake

The two clauses can conflict: if a school accommodates religion too much it may violate establishment; if it restricts religion too much it may violate free exercise.

2Required Case: Engel v. Vitale

REQUIRED CASE

Engel v. Vitale (1962)

6–1
First Amendment — establishment clause
Facts
New York's Board of Regents wrote a short, 'nondenominational' prayer for public school students to recite each morning: 'Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee...' Participation was voluntary. Parents, including Steven Engel, sued.
Constitutional question
Does a voluntary, nondenominational, school-sponsored prayer violate the establishment clause?
Decision
Yes. Justice Hugo Black: government-written and government-sponsored prayer in public schools is unconstitutional, even if voluntary and nondenominational. 'It is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers.'
Why it matters
Ended official prayer in public schools; reinforced the wall of separation. Followed by Abington v. Schempp (1963, Bible readings).
Memory hook: Engel = Establishment clause. No school-written prayer.

3Required Case: Wisconsin v. Yoder

REQUIRED CASE

Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972)

7–0
First Amendment — free exercise clause
Facts
Amish parents (Jonas Yoder and others) refused to send their children to school after 8th grade, violating Wisconsin's law requiring attendance until 16. Amish beliefs held that high school would expose children to worldly values.
Constitutional question
Does a compulsory education law violate Amish parents' free exercise of religion?
Decision
Yes. Chief Justice Burger: the parents' religious freedom outweighed the state's interest in two extra years of schooling; the Amish way of life provided adequate vocational training.
Why it matters
Strong protection for free exercise of religion; individual religious liberty can outweigh state laws.
Memory hook: Yoder = Yes to religious freedom for the Amish (free exercise).

4Other Key Religion Cases

1879
Reynolds v. U.S. — polygamy ban upheld: belief protected, action not
1971
Lemon v. Kurtzman — Lemon test: a law must have a secular purpose, neither advance nor inhibit religion, and avoid excessive entanglement
1990
Employment Division v. Smith — neutral laws (drug laws) can burden religion → Congress passed RFRA (1993)
2002
Zelman v. Simmons-Harris — Cleveland voucher program upheld (money goes to parents)
2022
Kennedy v. Bremerton — coach's personal post-game prayer protected; Court abandoned the Lemon test for 'history and tradition'

5Contemporary First Amendment Issues

  • Public funding of religious schools: generally limited, though vouchers and aid to religious colleges have been upheld.
  • Religion in public schools: formal prayer and even organized moments of silence (Wallace v. Jaffree, 1985) and student-led prayer at football games (Santa Fe v. Doe, 2000) were struck down. Popular opinion still largely supports school prayer (Gallup 2014: 61%).
  • Religious displays on public land: depends on context (Ten Commandments, crosses).
Update since the book (2021)

The Roberts Court has strengthened free exercise claims (e.g., Carson v. Makin, 2022, religious schools can't be excluded from state tuition aid).
