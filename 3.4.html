Topic 3.4

First Amendment: Freedom of the Press

Essential QuestionTo what extent does the Supreme Court's interpretation of freedom of the press reflect a commitment to individual liberty?
In one minute

A free press keeps government accountable. The Court strongly opposes prior restraint (government censorship before publication) — Near v. Minnesota (1931) and the required case New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), the Pentagon Papers case. New York Times v. Sullivan (1964) made it hard for public officials to win libel suits by requiring proof of 'malicious intent' (actual malice).

1Free Press in a Democracy

“Freedom of the press and constitutional liberty must live or perish together.”

— Justice Hugo Black

Prior restraint

Government blocking material before it's published. Almost always unconstitutional — the government bears a 'heavy burden.'

Libel

False written statements that damage a reputation (slander = spoken). Not protected.

Malicious intent (actual malice)

Public officials must prove the publisher knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for the truth.

'Breathing space'

The press needs room to make honest mistakes without being sued into silence (Sullivan).

2Key Press Cases

1931
Near v. Minnesota — struck down the 'Minnesota Gag Law'; prior restraint unconstitutional; press freedom applies to states (incorporation)
1964
New York Times v. Sullivan — public officials must prove actual malice to win libel suits
1971
New York Times Co. v. United States — Pentagon Papers could be published
1988
Hazelwood v. Kuhlmeier — schools may edit school-sponsored newspapers

3Required Case: New York Times Co. v. United States

REQUIRED CASE

New York Times Co. v. United States (1971)

6–3
First Amendment — freedom of the press (prior restraint)
Facts
Military analyst Daniel Ellsberg leaked the classified Pentagon Papers, a secret history of U.S. decision-making in Vietnam, to the New York Times and Washington Post. The Nixon administration sought an injunction to stop publication, citing national security.
Constitutional question
Can the government prevent newspapers from publishing classified documents (prior restraint) to protect national security?
Decision
No. In a short per curiam opinion, the Court said the government had not met the 'heavy burden' needed to justify prior restraint. Justice Black: the press serves 'the governed, not the governors.'
Why it matters
Strong presumption against prior restraint; the press can publish even sensitive material unless the government proves serious, immediate harm.
Memory hook: NYT v. US = No You Tan't stop the presses (Pentagon Papers).

AP Exam Tip

Political cartoons and satire are protected too — Hustler Magazine v. Falwell (1988) extended the actual-malice standard to parody of public figures.
