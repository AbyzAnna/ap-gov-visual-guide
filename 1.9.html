Topic 1.9

Federalism in Action

Essential QuestionHow does the distribution of powers among three federal branches and between national and state governments impact policymaking?
In one minute

Because power is split among three branches AND two levels of government, policymaking is a shared and contested process. This creates multiple access points for citizens and interest groups, lets states act as 'laboratories of democracy,' and produces conflicts — over marijuana, the environment, and education — between federal and state law.

Policy rarely comes from one place. Citizens and groups who lose in one arena (Congress) can try another (a state legislature, a federal agency, the courts). That's why the U.S. has so many access points.

2Policy Case Studies

Drug policy

Marijuana

The Controlled Substances Act (1970) makes marijuana illegal federally, yet many states have legalized medical or recreational use. Federal law is supreme (Gonzales v. Raich, 2005), but federal enforcement priorities vary by administration.

Environment

Environment

Clean Air Act (1970), Clean Water Act (1972), Endangered Species Act (1973), National Environmental Policy Act: federal standards that states implement. States like California set stricter auto-emission rules. Internationally: Kyoto Protocol (1997) (U.S. never ratified) and Paris Agreement (2015) (U.S. joined, left, rejoined).

Education

Education

Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965) → No Child Left Behind (2002) (federal testing, accountability) → Every Student Succeeds Act (2015) (returned control to states). States and local districts still provide most school funding.

Civil rights

Same-sex marriage

States moved first (Massachusetts, 2004); Congress had passed DOMA (1996); the Court decided U.S. v. Windsor (2013) and Obergefell (2015).

Update since the book (2021)

Since 2021: the U.S. left the Paris Agreement again in 2025, and federal marijuana rescheduling has been debated. The pattern the book teaches — states and the national government pulling in different directions — still applies.

3Political Participation and Policymaking

  • States as 'laboratories of democracy' (Justice Brandeis): a policy can be tried in one state before going national (welfare-to-work, health care in Massachusetts → ACA).
  • Interest groups lobby at every level and use litigation to move policy.
  • Citizens use initiatives and referendums in states (e.g., marijuana legalization ballot measures).
  • Divided government in Washington pushes activists toward the states.

“Pollution doesn't respect state boundaries, and it is difficult if not impossible for states acting alone to deal with it.”

Use education as evidence for both expanding federal power (NCLB) and devolution (ESSA).
