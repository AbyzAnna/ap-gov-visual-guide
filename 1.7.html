Topic 1.7

Relationship Between the States and Federal Government

Essential QuestionHow do the needs of society affect the allocation of power between national and state governments?
In one minute

Federalism divides power between the national and state governments. Some powers are exclusive to the nation, some are reserved to states, and some are concurrent (shared). Over time, the balance has shifted with society's needs, especially through money: grants-in-aid, categorical and block grants, and mandates (fiscal federalism).

1Who Has Which Powers?

States also keep police powers — authority to protect the health, safety, welfare, and morals of residents (criminal law, schools, marriage, zoning).

2Article IV: How States Treat Each Other

Full faith and credit

States must recognize the public acts, records, and court decisions of other states (marriages, divorces, driver's licenses, contracts).

Privileges and immunities

A state can't unreasonably discriminate against citizens of other states (e.g., access to courts, travel, owning property).

Extradition

A state must return a fugitive to the state where the crime was committed.

From the book

The Defense of Marriage Act (1996) said states didn't have to recognize same-sex marriages from other states — a full-faith-and-credit fight later resolved by Obergefell v. Hodges (2015).

3Changing Models of Federalism

1789–1930s
Dual federalism — 'layer cake': national and state governments are separate, each supreme in its own sphere.
1930s–1960s
Cooperative federalism — 'marble cake': the New Deal and Great Society blend national & state roles; federal money flows to states.
1970s
Revenue sharing (Nixon) — federal money to states with few strings; ended 1986.
1980s–1990s
Devolution / New Federalism — returning power to states (Reagan; 1996 welfare reform).
Today
Fiscal federalism — the federal government uses money (grants & mandates) to shape state policy.

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (1996) converted welfare into block grants (TANF), a classic example of devolution. The Unfunded Mandates Reform Act (1995) tried to limit unfunded mandates.

AP Exam Tip

States prefer block grants (flexibility); Congress often prefers categorical grants (control and credit). This is a favorite FRQ comparison.
