Topic 2.14

Holding the Bureaucracy Accountable

Essential QuestionHow is the bureaucracy held accountable by congressional oversight and by the president in carrying out goals of the administration?
In one minute

The bureaucracy is accountable to Congress through congressional oversight — hearings, investigations, authorization of spending and appropriations (the power of the purse) — and to the president through appointments, executive orders, budget proposals, and the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA).

On this page:Congressional OversightThe President and the BureaucracyKey termsQuick check

1Congressional Oversight

Oversight hearings

Committees question agency heads; can subpoena documents and witnesses (e.g., hearings on the IRS or Secret Service).

Authorization of spending

A law creating or continuing a program and setting a spending ceiling.

Appropriations

The actual money each year — Congress can increase, cut, or zero out an agency's budget (power of the purse).

Rewriting laws

Congress can change an agency's mission or reorganize it (e.g., creating DHS in 2002).

Confirmation

Senate confirms agency heads.

GAO & inspectors general

Audit and investigate waste and fraud.

AP Exam Tip

Authorization ≠ appropriation: authorization says a program can exist and spend up to X; appropriation actually gives the money.

2The President and the Bureaucracy

  • Appoints and can fire most agency heads (independent commissioners have fixed terms).
  • Issues executive orders directing how laws are enforced.
  • Proposes the agency budget through the Office of Management and Budget (OMB).
  • OIRA (inside OMB, since 1980) reviews major regulations for costs and benefits.
  • Can reorganize agencies (with congressional approval).

Presidents' goals sometimes clash with career staff; agencies may move slowly ('bureaucratic drift'). Courts can also hold agencies accountable when they exceed their authority. Compliance monitoring ensures agencies' rules are followed.
