Topic 1.5

Ratification of the U.S. Constitution

Essential QuestionWhat was the ongoing impact of political negotiation and compromise at the Constitutional Convention on the development of the constitutional system?
In one minute

In Philadelphia in 1787, 55 delegates replaced the Articles instead of fixing them. The new Constitution was the product of compromises: the Great (Connecticut) Compromise on representation, the Three-Fifths Compromise on enslaved people, the Electoral College, and the promise of a Bill of Rights. Article V created a difficult but possible amendment process.

2The Major Compromises

Representation

Great (Connecticut) Compromise

Bicameral Congress: the House based on population (elected by people), the Senate with 2 per state (chosen by state legislatures until the 17th Amendment). Revenue bills start in the House. Worked out by the Grand Committee.

Slavery

Three-Fifths Compromise

Each enslaved person counted as 3/5 of a person for both representation and direct taxation — boosting Southern power in the House and Electoral College. Also: the slave trade could not be banned until 1808; fugitive slave clause.

Choosing the president

Electoral College

President chosen by electors equal to each state's House + Senate seats — a compromise between election by Congress and by popular vote.

Trade

Commerce compromise

Congress may regulate commerce and tax imports, but no taxes on exports (protecting Southern agriculture).

Remember

Slavery was 'resolved' by compromise rather than abolition — a decision that shaped politics until the Civil War and the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments.

4The Amendment Process (Article V)

The amendment process requires a supermajority at both stages, reflecting federalism (states must agree) and making change deliberate. Only 27 amendments have been ratified; the first ten are the Bill of Rights (1791).

5Constitutional Debates — Then and Now

The framers left some issues open, and compromise continues to drive debate:

Electoral College

Winners of the popular vote lost the presidency in 2000 and 2016 — renewing calls for reform.

Federal vs state power in education

No Child Left Behind (2002) expanded federal testing mandates; Race to the Top (2009) used grants to push reforms; Every Student Succeeds Act (2015) returned more control to states.

Security vs liberty

The USA PATRIOT Act (2001) expanded government surveillance after 9/11 — raising Fourth Amendment concerns.

AP Exam Tip

Know who the stakeholders are in a debate (large vs small states, North vs South, federal vs state officials). AP questions ask how compromise shaped an institution.
