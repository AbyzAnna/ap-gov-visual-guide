Topic 2.3

Congressional Behavior

Essential QuestionHow is congressional behavior influenced by election processes, partisanship, and divided government?
In one minute

Members of Congress balance the wishes of constituents, their own judgment, and their party — the delegate, trustee, and politico models. Behavior is shaped by how districts are drawn (gerrymandering, limited by Baker v. Carr and Shaw v. Reno), by rising partisanship, and by divided government, which often leads to gridlock.

1Models of Representation

Voters decide

Delegate model

Votes the way constituents want, even against personal views. 'My district wants it, so I vote yes.'

Member decides

Trustee model

Uses own best judgment for the public good (Edmund Burke).

Mix

Politico model

Blends both — acts as a delegate on issues voters care strongly about, as a trustee on others.

2Redistricting & Gerrymandering

After each census, state legislatures redraw House district lines. Gerrymandering = drawing lines to benefit a party or group, through packing (concentrating opponents into one district) and cracking (splitting them across many).

Racial gerrymandering draws lines mainly based on race. Majority-minority districts (where a minority group is the majority) were created under the Voting Rights Act to boost minority representation. A swing district is competitive; fewer of these exist because of gerrymandering and political sorting.

3Required Cases on Representation

REQUIRED CASE

Baker v. Carr (1962)

6–2
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection clause
Facts
Tennessee had not redrawn its state legislative districts since 1901 despite huge population shifts to cities. Urban voters like Charles Baker had far less voting weight than rural voters.
Constitutional question
Can federal courts hear cases about legislative apportionment, or is this a 'political question' for the legislature?
Decision
Courts can hear redistricting cases — they are 'justiciable' under the equal protection clause. Follow-up cases (Reynolds v. Sims, 1964; Wesberry v. Sanders, 1964) set the 'one person, one vote' principle: districts must have roughly equal populations.
Why it matters
Opened the door to judicial review of districting; forced states to redraw maps based on population; shifted power from rural areas to cities and suburbs.
Memory hook: Baker = Balanced districts: one person, one vote.
REQUIRED CASE

Shaw v. Reno (1993)

5–4
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection clause
Facts
North Carolina drew a second majority-Black congressional district (District 12) that snaked ~160 miles along Interstate 85 — sometimes no wider than the highway.
Constitutional question
Does a district drawn mainly on the basis of race violate equal protection?
Decision
Yes, it can. Districts drawn primarily based on race are subject to strict scrutiny and must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. Justice O'Connor said the map resembled 'political apartheid.'
Why it matters
Limits racial gerrymandering even when intended to help minority voters; race can't be the predominant factor.
Memory hook: Shaw = the 'snake' district shows race can't be the main shape-maker.

Update since the book (2021)

Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that partisan gerrymandering claims are political questions federal courts won't decide; Allen v. Milligan (2023) upheld Voting Rights Act Section 2 protections for minority voters in Alabama.

Gridlock

little legislation; fights over budgets & nominations

  • Divided government makes confirmations harder (e.g., the Senate refused to consider Merrick Garland in 2016).
  • A 'lame duck' president (after a successor is elected or near term limit) has less influence.
  • Congress's approval rating is often below 20%, yet most incumbents win reelection — the 'love my member, hate Congress' paradox.
