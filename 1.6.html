Topic 1.6

Principles of American Government

Essential QuestionWhat do the principles of separation of powers and 'checks and balances' mean to the U.S. political system?
In one minute

The Constitution divides power among three branches (separation of powers) and gives each branch tools to limit the others (checks and balances). Madison explains the logic in Federalist No. 51: 'ambition must be made to counteract ambition.' The result is a system with many access points for influence — and many ways to block action.

1Separation of Powers vs Checks and Balances

Separation of powers

  • Each branch has its OWN job
  • Legislative makes laws (Art. I)
  • Executive enforces laws (Art. II)
  • Judicial interprets laws (Art. III)
  • Idea from Montesquieu
VS

Checks and balances

  • Each branch can LIMIT the others
  • Veto, override, impeachment
  • Advice and consent, judicial review
  • Prevents any one branch from dominating
  • Idea from Madison / Fed 51

3Federalist No. 51

FOUNDATIONAL DOCUMENT

Federalist No. 51

James Madison · 1788 · Required foundational document
Big argument: Because people are not angels, government must be structured so that branches check each other — separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism protect liberty.
  • Each branch should have 'a will of its own' and be as independent as possible.
  • 'Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.' — give officials the constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments.
  • The legislature naturally dominates in a republic, so divide it into two houses (bicameralism).
  • Compound republic: power is divided between national and state governments, then among branches — a 'double security' for rights.
  • Like Fed 10: a large society with many interests protects minorities.

“If men were angels, no government would be necessary.”

4The Three Branches in Practice

In practice the branches overlap and compete. Presidents use executive orders when Congress won't act; Congress uses hearings and budgets to push back; courts settle disputes between them. Multiple access points (Congress, agencies, courts, states) give interest groups and citizens many places to influence policy — but also make it easy for stakeholders to block change.

AP Exam Tip

If an FRQ asks how one branch checks another, name the specific check (e.g., 'the Senate can refuse to confirm a nominee') — not just 'Congress checks the president.'
