Topic 2.1

Congress: The Senate and the House of Representatives

Essential QuestionWhat are the structures, powers, and functions of each house of Congress?
In one minute

Congress is bicameral: a 435-member House of Representatives based on population and a 100-member Senate with two senators per state. Article I gives Congress enumerated powers (tax, spend, declare war, regulate commerce) plus implied powers through the necessary and proper clause. Each chamber also has unique powers.

Seats in the House are reapportioned among states after each census (every 10 years); state legislatures then redraw district lines (redistricting).

2Powers of Congress

Enumerated (expressed) powers

Listed in Art. I, Sec. 8: lay & collect taxes, borrow money, regulate interstate & foreign commerce, coin money, establish post offices, declare war, raise army & navy, establish lower courts, naturalization.

Implied powers

Via the necessary and proper clause: create a national bank, set a minimum wage, draft soldiers, create agencies.

Power of the purse

Only Congress can spend federal money and raise revenue — its strongest check on the executive.

Non-legislative powers

Impeachment, confirming appointments, ratifying treaties, proposing amendments, oversight, choosing a president if no Electoral College majority.

From the book

War Powers Act (1973): Congress tried to reclaim war powers after Vietnam — the president must notify Congress within 48 hours of sending troops and withdraw within 60 days (plus 30) without authorization.

3Differing Powers for House and Senate

Only the HOUSE

  • Starts all revenue (tax) bills
  • Impeaches (brings charges) by majority vote
  • Chooses the president if no Electoral College majority
  • Rules Committee controls debate
VS

Only the SENATE

  • Advice and consent: confirms presidential appointments (judges, cabinet, ambassadors)
  • Ratifies treaties (2/3 vote)
  • Tries impeachments; 2/3 to remove
  • Chooses the VP if no Electoral College majority
  • Unlimited debate (filibuster)
AP Exam Tip

Members of Congress can form caucuses (groups with shared interests, e.g., Congressional Black Caucus, Freedom Caucus) and coalitions to pass legislation.
