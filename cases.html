Full case cards

Topic 1.8
REQUIRED CASE

McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

9–0
Necessary and proper clause · Supremacy clause
Facts
Congress created the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland taxed the bank's Baltimore branch; cashier James McCulloch refused to pay.
Constitutional question
Can Congress create a national bank when that power is not listed? Can a state tax a federal institution?
Decision
Yes and no. Chief Justice John Marshall: the necessary and proper clause gives Congress implied powers to carry out enumerated ones (taxing, borrowing, regulating commerce). And because of the supremacy clause, a state cannot tax the national government — 'the power to tax involves the power to destroy.'
Why it matters
Established implied powers and national supremacy; laid the foundation for a strong national government and broad congressional power.
Memory hook: McCulloch = More power for the Main (national) government. 'Power to tax is the power to destroy.'
Topic 1.8
REQUIRED CASE

United States v. Lopez (1995)

5–4
Commerce clause · Tenth Amendment
Facts
Alfonso Lopez, a 12th-grader in San Antonio, brought a handgun to school and was charged under the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act (1990), which Congress passed using its commerce power.
Constitutional question
Does the commerce clause allow Congress to ban guns near schools?
Decision
No. Chief Justice Rehnquist: carrying a gun near a school is not economic activity and does not 'substantially affect' interstate commerce. Upholding the law would give Congress a general police power that belongs to the states.
Why it matters
First time since the New Deal that the Court limited the commerce clause — a win for federalism / states' rights and limits on Congress.
Memory hook: Lopez = Limits on Congress. Guns in school ≠ commerce.
Topic 2.3
REQUIRED CASE

Baker v. Carr (1962)

6–2
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection clause
Facts
Tennessee had not redrawn its state legislative districts since 1901 despite huge population shifts to cities. Urban voters like Charles Baker had far less voting weight than rural voters.
Constitutional question
Can federal courts hear cases about legislative apportionment, or is this a 'political question' for the legislature?
Decision
Courts can hear redistricting cases — they are 'justiciable' under the equal protection clause. Follow-up cases (Reynolds v. Sims, 1964; Wesberry v. Sanders, 1964) set the 'one person, one vote' principle: districts must have roughly equal populations.
Why it matters
Opened the door to judicial review of districting; forced states to redraw maps based on population; shifted power from rural areas to cities and suburbs.
Memory hook: Baker = Balanced districts: one person, one vote.
Topic 2.3
REQUIRED CASE

Shaw v. Reno (1993)

5–4
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection clause
Facts
North Carolina drew a second majority-Black congressional district (District 12) that snaked ~160 miles along Interstate 85 — sometimes no wider than the highway.
Constitutional question
Does a district drawn mainly on the basis of race violate equal protection?
Decision
Yes, it can. Districts drawn primarily based on race are subject to strict scrutiny and must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest. Justice O'Connor said the map resembled 'political apartheid.'
Why it matters
Limits racial gerrymandering even when intended to help minority voters; race can't be the predominant factor.
Memory hook: Shaw = the 'snake' district shows race can't be the main shape-maker.
Topic 2.8
REQUIRED CASE

Marbury v. Madison (1803)

4–0
Article III · Judiciary Act of 1789
Facts
On his way out, President John Adams appointed William Marbury as a justice of the peace ('midnight judges'). The commission wasn't delivered. New Secretary of State James Madison (under Jefferson) refused to deliver it. Marbury asked the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus under the Judiciary Act of 1789.
Constitutional question
Does the Supreme Court have the power to order Madison to deliver the commission?
Decision
Chief Justice John Marshall: Marbury deserved his commission, but the part of the Judiciary Act giving the Supreme Court power to issue the writ in its original jurisdiction expanded Article III unconstitutionally — so it was void. The Court can't issue the writ.
Why it matters
Established judicial review: courts can declare acts of Congress unconstitutional. Made the judiciary a co-equal branch.
Memory hook: Marbury = Marshall Makes judicial review. Lost the battle (no writ), won the war (power).
Topic 3.2
REQUIRED CASE

Engel v. Vitale (1962)

6–1
First Amendment — establishment clause
Facts
New York's Board of Regents wrote a short, 'nondenominational' prayer for public school students to recite each morning: 'Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee...' Participation was voluntary. Parents, including Steven Engel, sued.
Constitutional question
Does a voluntary, nondenominational, school-sponsored prayer violate the establishment clause?
Decision
Yes. Justice Hugo Black: government-written and government-sponsored prayer in public schools is unconstitutional, even if voluntary and nondenominational. 'It is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers.'
Why it matters
Ended official prayer in public schools; reinforced the wall of separation. Followed by Abington v. Schempp (1963, Bible readings).
Memory hook: Engel = Establishment clause. No school-written prayer.
Topic 3.2
REQUIRED CASE

Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972)

7–0
First Amendment — free exercise clause
Facts
Amish parents (Jonas Yoder and others) refused to send their children to school after 8th grade, violating Wisconsin's law requiring attendance until 16. Amish beliefs held that high school would expose children to worldly values.
Constitutional question
Does a compulsory education law violate Amish parents' free exercise of religion?
Decision
Yes. Chief Justice Burger: the parents' religious freedom outweighed the state's interest in two extra years of schooling; the Amish way of life provided adequate vocational training.
Why it matters
Strong protection for free exercise of religion; individual religious liberty can outweigh state laws.
Memory hook: Yoder = Yes to religious freedom for the Amish (free exercise).
Topic 3.3
REQUIRED CASE

Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District (1969)

7–2
First Amendment — free speech (symbolic speech)
Facts
Students John and Mary Beth Tinker and Christopher Eckhardt wore black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War. The school had banned armbands and suspended them.
Constitutional question
Does banning armbands as a form of protest violate students' free speech?
Decision
Yes. Justice Abe Fortas: students don't 'shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.' Schools may restrict speech only if it causes a substantial disruption; the silent armbands didn't.
Why it matters
Protects symbolic speech and student expression; set the 'substantial disruption' standard for schools.
Memory hook: Tinker = Teen armbands; rights don't stop at the schoolhouse gate.
Topic 3.3
REQUIRED CASE

Schenck v. United States (1919)

9–0
First Amendment — free speech
Facts
During World War I, Socialist Party official Charles Schenck mailed leaflets urging men to resist the draft. He was convicted under the Espionage Act of 1917.
Constitutional question
Does the Espionage Act violate Schenck's free speech?
Decision
No. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes: speech can be punished if it creates a 'clear and present danger' of bringing about evils Congress has a right to prevent — especially in wartime. 'Free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic.'
Why it matters
First major free-speech case; established that speech can be limited. Later narrowed by Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969): only speech inciting imminent lawless action can be banned.
Memory hook: Schenck = Shout 'fire' in a theater — speech can be limited (clear & present danger).
Topic 3.4
REQUIRED CASE

New York Times Co. v. United States (1971)

6–3
First Amendment — freedom of the press (prior restraint)
Facts
Military analyst Daniel Ellsberg leaked the classified Pentagon Papers, a secret history of U.S. decision-making in Vietnam, to the New York Times and Washington Post. The Nixon administration sought an injunction to stop publication, citing national security.
Constitutional question
Can the government prevent newspapers from publishing classified documents (prior restraint) to protect national security?
Decision
No. In a short per curiam opinion, the Court said the government had not met the 'heavy burden' needed to justify prior restraint. Justice Black: the press serves 'the governed, not the governors.'
Why it matters
Strong presumption against prior restraint; the press can publish even sensitive material unless the government proves serious, immediate harm.
Memory hook: NYT v. US = No You Tan't stop the presses (Pentagon Papers).
Topic 3.7
REQUIRED CASE

McDonald v. Chicago (2010)

5–4
Second Amendment · Fourteenth Amendment due process clause
Facts
Chicago banned handgun possession by most private citizens. Otis McDonald, a retired Black custodian in a high-crime neighborhood, wanted a handgun at home for self-defense.
Constitutional question
Does the Second Amendment right recognized in Heller apply to states and cities through the Fourteenth Amendment?
Decision
Yes. Justice Alito: the right to keep and bear arms for self-defense is fundamental and is incorporated through the 14th Amendment's due process clause. (Justice Thomas concurred using the privileges or immunities clause.)
Why it matters
Extended Heller to every state and city; a classic example of selective incorporation limiting state power.
Memory hook: McDonald = Make it apply to Different governments (states & cities).
Topic 3.8
REQUIRED CASE

Gideon v. Wainwright (1963)

9–0
Sixth Amendment right to counsel · Fourteenth Amendment due process
Facts
Clarence Earl Gideon was charged with breaking into a Florida pool hall — a felony. Too poor to hire a lawyer, he asked for one; Florida only appointed counsel in capital cases. He defended himself, was convicted, and wrote a handwritten petition from prison to the Supreme Court.
Constitutional question
Does the Sixth Amendment's right to counsel apply to state felony defendants who can't afford a lawyer?
Decision
Yes. Justice Black: the right to counsel is fundamental to a fair trial and is incorporated through the 14th Amendment. States must provide lawyers to poor defendants in felony cases. (Overturned Betts v. Brady, 1942.) Gideon was retried with a lawyer and acquitted.
Why it matters
Created the public defender system; a landmark of selective incorporation and procedural due process.
Memory hook: Gideon = Gets a lawyer (6th Amendment), even if poor.
Topic 3.9
REQUIRED CASE

Roe v. Wade (1973)

7–2
Fourteenth Amendment due process clause — right to privacy
Facts
'Jane Roe' (Norma McCorvey), a pregnant single woman in Texas, challenged a state law banning abortion except to save the mother's life.
Constitutional question
Does the Constitution's right to privacy protect a woman's decision to have an abortion?
Decision
Yes. Justice Harry Blackmun: the right to privacy (from Griswold, via 14th Am. due process) includes the abortion decision, balanced against state interests that grow during pregnancy — the trimester framework: 1st trimester, no state ban; 2nd, regulation for the mother's health; 3rd (viability), states may ban except to protect the mother's life or health.
Why it matters
Extended privacy/substantive due process; became one of the most divisive rulings in U.S. history, mobilizing social movements on both sides.
Memory hook: Roe = Right to privacy Reaches abortion (trimesters).
Topic 3.11
REQUIRED CASE

Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, Kansas (1954)

9–0
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection clause
Facts
Linda Brown, a Black third-grader in Topeka, had to travel far to a segregated Black school instead of the nearby white school. The NAACP, led by Thurgood Marshall, combined her case with others from four states. They used social-science evidence (the Clarks' 'doll test') showing segregation harmed Black children.
Constitutional question
Does racial segregation in public schools violate the equal protection clause?
Decision
Yes. Chief Justice Earl Warren, for a unanimous Court: 'Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.' Overturned Plessy for public education. Brown II (1955) ordered desegregation 'with all deliberate speed.'
Why it matters
Ended legal segregation in schools; sparked the modern civil rights movement; a model of the Court protecting minority rights against majorities.
Memory hook: Brown = Breaks 'separate but equal.' Unanimous, 9–0.

Supporting (non-required) cases

Topic 3.5
Supporting case

District of Columbia v. Heller (2008)

5–4
Second Amendment
Facts
Washington, D.C. banned handguns and required other guns in homes to be disassembled or trigger-locked. Special police officer Dick Heller challenged it.
Constitutional question
Does the Second Amendment protect an individual right to possess firearms, unconnected to militia service?
Decision
Yes. Justice Scalia: individuals have a right to keep a handgun in the home for self-defense. But the right is not unlimited — bans on felons possessing guns or on guns in schools remain valid.
Why it matters
First ruling clearly recognizing an individual right. Since D.C. is federal, it didn't yet apply to states — that came in McDonald.
Memory hook: Heller = Handgun at Home is protected (federal enclave).
Topic 3.11
Supporting case

Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)

7–1
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection
Facts
Homer Plessy, who was 7/8 white, sat in a whites-only rail car in Louisiana to test the Separate Car Act and was arrested.
Constitutional question
Does state-mandated racial segregation violate equal protection?
Decision
No — 'separate but equal' facilities are constitutional. Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone: 'Our Constitution is color-blind.'
Why it matters
Legalized Jim Crow segregation for nearly 60 years until Brown.
Memory hook: Plessy = Permitted segregation.
Topic 3.12
Supporting case

Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education (1971)

9–0
Fourteenth Amendment — equal protection
Facts
Despite Brown, Charlotte, North Carolina schools remained largely segregated because of housing patterns.
Constitutional question
Can federal courts order busing to achieve racial integration?
Decision
Yes. Chief Justice Burger, unanimously: busing, racial ratios, and redrawn attendance zones are acceptable tools to remedy segregation.
Why it matters
Gave courts power to actively enforce Brown; sparked fierce public opposition to 'forced busing.'
Memory hook: Swann = School buses integrate.
Topic 3.13
Supporting case

Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978)

5–4
Fourteenth Amendment equal protection · Civil Rights Act Title VI
Facts
Allan Bakke, a white applicant, was rejected twice by UC Davis Medical School, which reserved 16 of 100 seats for minority students. His scores were higher than some admitted under the special program.
Constitutional question
Does a racial quota in admissions violate equal protection?
Decision
Quotas are unconstitutional — Bakke was admitted — BUT Justice Powell said race may be considered as one factor to achieve diversity.
Why it matters
Set the framework for decades: no quotas, but holistic consideration of race allowed.
Memory hook: Bakke = Ban quotas, but race can be a factor.
